## 1. How We Name Plays

**Every play name is exactly two words: a COLOR word + an ACTION word.**

```
   COLOR   +   ACTION
   (where)     (what)

   BLUE    =  the RIGHT side of the field (offense facing downfield)
   ORANGE  =  the LEFT side of the field (offense facing downfield)
```

### The color words (direction) — FIXED TO THE FIELD, NOT FLIPPED

> **New rule this season: BLUE and ORANGE are now permanently tied to a side of the field, not to a cone that moves.** **BLUE is always the offense's RIGHT side. ORANGE is always the offense's LEFT side.** This is true no matter which end zone we are attacking — the colors do not flip when we change ends.

- **BLUE** = the offense's **RIGHT** side, facing downfield.
- **ORANGE** = the offense's **LEFT** side, facing downfield.

**Why we changed this:** the old version of this book used a physical cone that we physically picked up and flipped to the other side every time we changed ends, specifically so BLUE always meant "the cone you can currently see," never a fixed direction. That worked, but it meant every coach and every parent had to re-flip their own mental picture of the field at halftime. Fixing BLUE = right and ORANGE = left removes that moving part: the colors now describe permanent zones of the field, not a landmark that moves with us.

> ⚠️ **Coaching note — the single most likely point of confusion at this age.** Because BLUE/ORANGE are now fixed to the field rather than flipped with our direction of attack, **players must learn to read "right" and "left" from the point of view of the offense facing downfield** — not from where they personally happen to be standing or facing at a given moment. A 6-7 year old mixing up his own left and right is completely normal, and this is now the #1 thing to drill and check before every game. **Recommended mitigation:** put a small blue or orange wristband/armband on each player's correct-side wrist (blue on the right wrist, orange on the left wrist) so a player can look down at his own body instead of doing the mental "which way am I facing" calculation — and/or plant a blue cone on the right sideline and an orange cone on the left sideline (from the offense's point of view, at both ends of the field) at every practice and every game so the sideline colors are a constant, visible landmark that never moves mid-game.

### The action words (what to do)

| Action word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **SWEEP** | Run wide and outside, fast. |
| **GHOST** | Fake one way, give it back the other way. |
| **BUBBLE** | Short catch behind the line, then run. |
| **DART** | Fake the run, then throw it downfield, fast and true. |

### Why we chose this convention

- **Kids at 6-7 mix up left and right.** Colors are still easier to hold onto and shout across a field than direction words — that part of the original reasoning hasn't changed, we've just fixed which side each color refers to instead of moving it around.
- **"Left" and "right" sound alike at a distance** and both have a hard "t" sound. **"Blue"** and **"Orange"** share no sounds at all — one syllable each, totally different mouth shape. You can shout them across a field.
- **Two words only.** A 6-year-old can hear it, repeat it, and hold it for the 30 seconds we have to snap the ball (Ref §4).
- **The name is the assignment.** "BLUE SWEEP" already tells the ball carrier where and how.
- Every action word starts with a **different letter and sound** — S, G, B, D — so no two calls blur together.

### Rule for naming NEW plays later

> **New play = existing COLOR word + a NEW ACTION word.**
> The new action word must be: **one or two syllables**, **start with a letter we are not already using**, and **describe the ball's path, not a person's name**.
> Never add a third word. Never add a number.

So the same play to the other side is free: **ORANGE SWEEP**, **BLUE GHOST**, **ORANGE BUBBLE**, **BLUE DART**. Learning four plays gets us eight calls.

## 2. Our Formation: STAR

**We line up in STAR on every single play.** Same spots, every time. The defense never learns anything from how we stand (Ref §17: with no blocking allowed, deception has to come from ball handling — so alignment must never tip the play).

### The 6 spots

| # | Spot | Where you find it (kid words) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **CENTER** | Stand on the ball. |
| 2 | **QB** | Stand **directly behind the Center, hands under him**, toes almost touching his heels. |
| 3 | **BLUE BACK** | Stand **directly behind the QB, one big step back**, then slide **one small step toward the blue cone** — a split-back look, tight and close to the Orange Back. |
| 4 | **ORANGE BACK** | Stand **directly behind the QB, one big step back**, then slide **one small step toward the orange cone** — mirrors the Blue Back, tight and close. |
| 5 | **BLUE WIDE** | Stand **on the blue sideline**, even with the ball. |
| 6 | **ORANGE WIDE** | Stand **on the orange sideline**, even with the ball. |

Landmarks are only: **the ball, the QB, and the two sidelines.** Nothing to measure. No coach walking anyone out.

> **Head coach change — split backfield.** The Blue Back and Orange Back now line up **close together, directly behind the QB** (one big step back, one small step apart to either side) instead of spread wide beside him. **Why:** with both backs tight and perfectly symmetric, the QB's fake and his real handoff look **identical** to the defense — same depth, same angle, same body language — because the mesh points sit almost on top of each other. The QB barely has to move to reach either back, which is exactly what makes a fake believable to a defense at this age.

### Why the QB is UNDER CENTER (not shotgun)

> **Head coach decision — keep this note for future coaches.** At 6-7 years old the shotgun snap is the least reliable thing on the field. A snap that hits the ground or sails wide is an **immediate dead play** — we lose the down before anyone runs a step. Under center the exchange is **hand-to-hand**: the Center hikes the ball straight up into the QB's hands between his legs. The ball never flies. That removes our single biggest source of lost downs.

**The exchange, in kid words:** QB puts both hands under the Center and **presses up**. Center **hikes the ball up into the QB's hands** — between the legs, never off to the side (Ref §6). QB **holds on with both hands** and does not move his feet until he feels the ball.

> ⚠️ **RULES NOTE — confirm with the league.** The packet says only that the ball **must be snapped between the legs, not off to one side** (Ref §6). It never says whether the ball must travel backward through the air, so it does **not** clearly state that a direct under-center hand-to-hand exchange is legal. A hand-to-hand hike still passes between the Center's legs, so we believe it complies, and the head coach's instruction governs — but **ask the league/referees to confirm before the first game** (Ref §16, item 21). If they say no, we go back to a short shotgun depth.

### The tradeoff we accept

Under center the QB **turns his back to the defense** on every handoff and sees the rush later than he would in shotgun. We accept that because rushers must start **7 yards off the line** (Ref §9), so there is real time before anyone arrives. Practical rule: **the QB never looks for the rush — he takes the snap, turns, gives the ball, and lets the ball carrier deal with the defense.**

### STAR diagram

```
   ORANGE SIDELINE (LEFT)                            BLUE SIDELINE (RIGHT)
   |                                                           |
   |  [OW]                    (C)                       [BW]   |
   |                     ------------------  <- line of scrimmage
   |                          (QB)   <- hands under Center
   |                        [OB][BB]   <- split backs, tight, one big step
   |                                      behind QB, one small step apart
   |                                                           |

   (C)  = Center  (on the ball)
   (QB) = Quarterback (DIRECTLY UNDER CENTER, hand-to-hand snap)
   [BB] = Blue Back (RIGHT)   [OB] = Orange Back (LEFT)  (SPLIT BACKFIELD — tight, close together)
   [BW] = Blue Wide (RIGHT)   [OW] = Orange Wide (LEFT)
```

### Why STAR works

- **Legal at 6-on-6** with a Center and a QB; the ball is hiked between the legs (Ref §6).
- **Two backs = fake handoffs.** Multi-back sets are our main misdirection tool (Ref §0, §17). The **tight split-back alignment** makes this stronger — both mesh points sit at the same depth and angle behind the QB, so a defense reading the QB's body cannot tell fake from real until the ball actually appears.
- **Two wides = real pass threat**, so a run look and a pass look are identical.
- **All 6 players are eligible receivers** (Ref §7), so nobody is a wasted body — including the Center, who has no blocking job (Ref §0.3).
- **Under center kills our worst failure** — no airborne snap to drop — and rushers still start **7 yards away** (Ref §9), so the QB has time even without a head start.

### Diagram conventions

> **Read every diagram in this book the same way, always.** **Downfield = up the page** — the direction we are attacking, toward the opponent's end zone. The QB, Center, and both Backs line up **below the line of scrimmage** on every diagram (closer to the bottom); the line of scrimmage marker sits in the middle; downfield is everything above it, toward the top. **Any time a ball carrier turns the corner, the arrow must end up pointing toward the top of the diagram** — never back toward the bottom, and never back across the line of scrimmage. This rule applies to every ASCII diagram below and to every SVG diagram (including the wristband mini-diagrams) in `Playbook/FNL-Playbook-Print.html`. In the ASCII diagrams, we mark the moment a runner turns upfield with a stacked `^ ^ ^` so the direction is never ambiguous in plain text.

### Wide receiver route — 5-step IN (all plays)

Both **BLUE WIDE** and **ORANGE WIDE** run the identical route on every play: release **straight upfield for 5 steps**, then **break sharply inward** toward the middle of the field, staying flat/level — never drifting back toward the line of scrimmage.

**Why we run it this way:** The in-breaking route carries the receiver **across the QB's field of vision**, which is exactly what a completable pass needs. More importantly for our run game, it **drags the cornerback in and away from the edge at the exact moment the running back is arriving at that edge** — one route clears the exact space BLUE SWEEP and ORANGE GHOST need to work. Later in the season, when we install more pass plays, this route is already drilled and installed, so it becomes an easy completion.

**Coaching point:** Receivers **count their own 5 steps out loud** — nobody paces it out for them. The break must be a sharp, flat cut inward; if a receiver drifts backward toward the line of scrimmage on the cut, he drags the defender right back into the running lane he is supposed to be clearing.

## 3. Play 1 — BLUE SWEEP

**Type: RUN.** Our base play.

```
   ORANGE SIDE (LEFT)     ^  UPFIELD / DOWNFIELD  ^          BLUE SIDE (RIGHT)
   |                      ^   (toward end zone)    ^                     |
   |             +--[OW]--+                +--[BW]--+                   |
   |            /   5 steps, then break IN   5 steps, then break IN  \   |
   |          [OW]                                              [BW]    |
   |           ^                                                    ^    |
   |    ------------------- LINE OF SCRIMMAGE -------------------        |
   |                            (QB)  <- hand-to-hand snap               |
   |                          [OB][BB]  <- tight split backs             |
   |                                                     ^ ^ ^           |
   |                        [BB]--->^ ^ ^  wide to blue side, THEN turns upfield |
   |        BB path: FIRST STEP is the whole play from this tight        |
   |        alignment — get wide FAST (toward blue/RIGHT side),          |
   |        then turn and run UPFIELD (^ toward top of diagram)          |
   |        OB fakes/runs the other way (toward orange/LEFT) as a decoy  |
```

### Assignments (one sentence each)

- **CENTER:** Hike the ball **up into the QB's hands** between your legs, then run five steps straight ahead and look for the ball.
- **QB:** Feel the ball, pull it in with both hands, **open your feet toward the blue cone** and put the ball in the Blue Back's belly on your **first step** — from the tight split-back spot the Blue Back is right there, so the mesh happens almost immediately.
- **BLUE BACK:** Take the ball and run **wide** toward the blue cone, then turn upfield toward the end zone — **your first step matters more now**: you start closer to the middle of the field than before, so a sharp, direct first step toward the cone is what gets you to the edge in time.
- **ORANGE BACK:** Run hard the **other way** toward the orange cone so they chase you — **step away cleanly** so you don't collide with the Blue Back out of the tight alignment.
- **BLUE WIDE:** Count **5 steps straight upfield**, then **break in** toward the middle, flat across the QB's vision.
- **ORANGE WIDE:** Count **5 steps straight upfield**, then **break in** toward the middle, flat across the QB's vision.

### When to call it

- First play of a series, and any time we need normal yards.
- **Not** inside a no-run zone: **5 yards off each end zone and 5 yards either side of midfield** (Ref §6). In those spots, call **BLUE BUBBLE**.
- **Tradeoff to know:** from the tighter split backfield, the Blue Back now starts **farther from the sideline** than in the old spread alignment, so it takes a beat longer to reach the edge. BLUE SWEEP is still our base play, but on an already-tight edge (fast HAWKS/LINE, or a short field) that extra beat is worth remembering when deciding whether to call it or lean on BLUE BUBBLE instead.

### What makes it fail

- QB leaves early — he pulls away before the Center hikes and the ball comes loose. **Hands stay under until you feel it.**
- QB turns the wrong way, or turns before securing the ball.
- The Blue Back turns upfield **too early** and runs into traffic instead of getting outside.
- The Blue Back and Orange Back **collide** stepping out of the tight alignment — clean, opposite-direction first steps fix this.
- Flag guarding — 10 yards and loss of down (Ref §11).
- Remember: once the ball is handed off, **every defender may rush** (Ref §9), so the edge closes fast — and it closes even faster now that the Blue Back starts closer to the middle.

### Coaching point

**"Get wide FIRST, then go north."** Reach the sideline, then turn upfield toward the end zone. To the QB: **"Feel it, first step, give."** The handoff comes even sooner now than before — the Blue Back must expect the ball almost immediately off the mesh. **First step clean, away from your brother back** — the two backs must not tangle up leaving the tight alignment. And **hands away from your flags** — we teach that before anything else (Ref §17). To the wides: **count your own 5 steps out loud**, then break in — don't let the in-cut drift backward toward the line of scrimmage.

## 4. Play 2 — ORANGE GHOST

**Type: RUN with a fake.** This is BLUE SWEEP's twin, and it only works after we have shown BLUE SWEEP.

```
   ORANGE SIDE (LEFT)     ^  UPFIELD / DOWNFIELD  ^          BLUE SIDE (RIGHT)
   |                      ^   (toward end zone)    ^                     |
   |             +--[OW]--+                +--[BW]--+                   |
   |            /   5 steps, then break IN   5 steps, then break IN  \   |
   |          [OW]                                              [BW]    |
   |           ^                                                    ^    |
   |    ------------------- LINE OF SCRIMMAGE -------------------        |
   |                            (QB)  <- hand-to-hand snap               |
   |                          [OB][BB]  <- tight split backs, same depth |
   |           ^ ^ ^                                        ^ ^ ^        |
   |   ^ ^ ^<--[OB]-->real handoff, OB turns   [BB]--fake-->^ ^ ^        |
   |   OB then turns UPFIELD to orange      (empty hands,                |
   |   real handoff on step 2               keep running) fake on step 1 |
   |                                         to BLUE side                |
   |   Both meshes happen at the SAME DEPTH, right behind the QB —       |
   |   from the defense's eyes, fake and real look identical.            |
```

### Assignments (one sentence each)

- **CENTER:** Hike the ball **up into the QB's hands** between your legs, then run five steps ahead and look for the ball.
- **QB:** Secure the snap, **open to the blue cone and fake** the ball to the Blue Back on your **first step**, then **spin back and hand it for real** to the Orange Back on your **second step** — the tighter mesh means both turns happen quicker and tighter together than before; sell **both** meshes at the exact same depth so they look like the same motion.
- **BLUE BACK:** Run the same path as BLUE SWEEP with **empty hands** and pretend you have the ball — **your first step still matters most**, since you start closer to the middle than before.
- **ORANGE BACK:** From the tight split spot, **barely move** on the fake — you're already right there — **count one quick beat** so the QB can finish faking to the Blue Back, then take the handoff and turn upfield toward the orange cone. The fake-to-real sequence is now **quicker and tighter** than it used to be.
- **BLUE WIDE:** Count **5 steps straight upfield**, then **break in** toward the middle, flat across the QB's vision.
- **ORANGE WIDE:** Count **5 steps straight upfield**, then **break in** toward the middle, flat across the QB's vision.

### When to call it

- After BLUE SWEEP has worked once or twice and the defense is flying to the blue side.
- Great on a second-down play when we need a chunk.
- **Not** in a no-run zone (Ref §6).
- **Tradeoff to know:** just like BLUE SWEEP, the Orange Back's real path starts farther from the sideline now, so the extra beat to reach the edge applies here too — ORANGE GHOST is still best called once the defense is already flowing hard to the blue side, since that flow buys back the time the tighter start costs.

### What makes it fail

- The Blue Back **stops running** after the fake — a lazy fake fools nobody.
- The QB rushes the mesh and the ball ends up on the ground. (Not a disaster: **there are no fumbles**, the ball is just spotted where it hits (Ref §10) — but we lose the play.)
- The QB starts turning **before** he has the ball. Under center there is no cushion — secure first, then move.
- The Orange Back leaves early and beats the QB to the spot. The second mesh comes **sooner** now that both backs are tight behind the QB, so the QB has less margin to finish the fake before the Orange Back is expecting the ball.
- The two backs **collide** stepping out of the tight alignment during the fake-then-real sequence — clean footwork on the first step from both backs prevents this.
- The QB fakes and hands off at **different depths** — if the fake mesh is shallower or deeper than the real mesh, the defense can read which one is live. **Sell both at the same depth.**

### Coaching point

**"The fake runs just as hard as the ball."** Both backs sprint. To the QB: **"Ball, fake, give — same depth, same look."** The fake is genuinely better under center — the QB's back hides the ball from the defense — and now the tight mesh makes fake and real nearly impossible to tell apart, but it needs a clean, repeatable mesh point at a **consistent depth**, so we rehearse this one at half speed a lot before we ever call it in a game (Ref §17). To the wides: **count your own 5 steps**, then break in — no drifting backward toward the line of scrimmage.

## 5. Play 3 — BLUE BUBBLE

**Type: PASS.** Short, easy, and **the receiver is already facing the QB before the ball comes** — that is the whole point at this age.

```
   ORANGE SIDE (LEFT)     ^  UPFIELD / DOWNFIELD  ^          BLUE SIDE (RIGHT)
   |                      ^   (toward end zone)    ^                     |
   |             +--[OW]--+                +--[BW]--+                   |
   |            /   5 steps, then break IN   5 steps, then break IN  \   |
   |          [OW]                                              [BW]    |
   |           ^                                                    ^    |
   |    ------------------- LINE OF SCRIMMAGE -------------------        |
   |                                 (QB) <- hand-to-hand snap           |
   |                                [OB][BB]  <- tight split backs        |
   |                                    |       [BB]--motion-->          |
   |            (starts from the tight spot)  [BB] stops HERE, turns and faces QB |
   |                                    |                     ^          |
   |            QB takes snap, steps STRAIGHT              short toss |  |
   |             BACK 2 steps, then throws        -----------------+     |
   |                    [OB] stays and faces QB (safety valve)           |
```

*Motion note: **only one player may be in motion at a time** (Ref §7), so the Blue Back is the only one moving before the snap.*

### Assignments (one sentence each)

- **CENTER:** Hike the ball **up into the QB's hands** between your legs, then run five steps ahead and turn around.
- **QB:** Secure the snap, **step straight back two steps** so you can see the field, then **toss the ball soft and low** to the Blue Back right away.
- **BLUE BACK:** Starting from the tight split-back spot, walk toward the blue cone before the snap, then **stop, turn around, and face the QB with your hands up**.
- **ORANGE BACK:** Stay in the tight spot directly behind the QB and face him — if the ball comes to you, catch it and run.
- **BLUE WIDE:** Count **5 steps straight upfield**, then **break in** toward the middle, flat across the QB's vision, to pull the cornerback away from the edge.
- **ORANGE WIDE:** Count **5 steps straight upfield**, then **break in** toward the middle, flat across the QB's vision, to pull the cornerback away from the edge.

### When to call it

- **Inside a no-run zone** — near either end zone or near midfield. Those spots must be pass plays (Ref §6, §17).
- Any time we need a first down and the defense has crowded the run.

### What makes it fail

- The QB holds the ball: **7-second pass clock**, and time-out means dead ball and loss of down (Ref §8). His two steps back must be **fast** — they are the only thing between the snap and the throw.
- The QB tries to throw while still standing on top of the Center. Get off the line first.
- The Blue Back is still moving/backpedaling when the ball arrives instead of set and facing.
- The Blue Back's pre-snap walk **crosses too close to the Orange Back** — starting from the tight split-back spot, the Blue Back must walk a clean path away from the Orange Back, not through him.
- A high or hard throw — this is a **soft toss**, not a heave. A shovel pass is legal too (Ref §8).
- Two players moving before the snap = **illegal motion, 5 yards and loss of down** (Ref §11).
- Catch rule: you need **at least one foot inbounds** (Ref §7), so don't drift onto the sideline.

### Coaching point

**"Turn around EARLY, hands up, eyes on the ball."** And to the QB: **"Ball, back-back, throw."** The pass is short on purpose; the yards come after the catch, not in the air. To the wides: **count your own 5 steps**, then break in flat — no drifting back toward the line of scrimmage.

## 6. Play 4 — ORANGE DART

**Type: PASS (play-action).** The hardest play in the book, and it only exists because we already run BLUE SWEEP, ORANGE GHOST, and the 5-step IN route on every snap. **Do not install this before the run game is reliable.** It is installed fourth (of five) — see §8.

### Why "DART"

The action word starts with **D** — unused (S, G, B are taken by SWEEP, GHOST, BUBBLE). **DART** is one syllable, sounds nothing like the other three when shouted across a field, and it describes the ball's path: a dart thrown fast and true downfield, after everyone believes it's a run. Per our naming rule, the new action word describes the ball's path, not a person (§1).

### The concept in one sentence

**ORANGE DART looks exactly like ORANGE GHOST for its first two steps, then the QB stops faking and throws to a receiver on his 5-step IN route instead of completing the handoff.**

### Play-action mechanic — it must be indistinguishable from ORANGE GHOST

- The **snap, the QB's first step (open to the blue cone, fake to Blue Back), and the Blue Back's empty-handed sell** are **identical, step for step, to ORANGE GHOST** (§4). This is the entire point: if the defense can tell ORANGE DART from ORANGE GHOST before the ball is out, the play is already dead.
- The difference happens on the QB's **second step**, the moment he would spin and hand to the Orange Back in ORANGE GHOST. Instead of completing that handoff, the QB **plants, brings the ball up, and throws** — there is **no real mesh point at all** this time; the Orange Back's job is to sell the mesh with his body exactly as if he is about to receive it, then release.
- **This is a fake handoff, not a handoff.** No ball ever changes hands to the Orange Back. That distinction matters directly for the defensive rush rule — see the Rules Compliance section below.

### ORANGE DART diagram

```
   ORANGE SIDE (LEFT)     ^  UPFIELD / DOWNFIELD  ^          BLUE SIDE (RIGHT)
   |                      ^   (toward end zone)    ^                     |
   |             +--[OW]--+                +--[BW]--+                   |
   |            /   5 steps, then break IN   5 steps, then break IN  \   |
   |          [OW]                                              [BW]    |
   |   <-- fine dash:                                          ^        |
   |       THROW HERE   :                                      :        |
   |    ------------------- LINE OF SCRIMMAGE -------------------        |
   |                            (QB)  <- hand-to-hand snap               |
   |                          [OB][BB]  <- tight split backs             |
   |            . . .                            ^ ^ ^                  |
   |   [OB] sells mesh, NO handoff,        ^ ^ ^ <--fake--[BB]           |
   |   releases as checkdown/safety     fake on step 1   (empty hands,   |
   |   valve on step 2 instead of       to BLUE side     keep running)   |
   |   taking a ball that never comes                                    |
   |                                                                     |
   |   QB plants on step 2 (same spot ORANGE GHOST hands off),           |
   |   comes up throwing a fine-dash pass ( . . . ) to ORANGE WIDE       |
   |   on his 5-step IN cut. BLUE WIDE is the same throw mirrored        |
   |   if the fake instead pulls defenders to the ORANGE/LEFT side.      |
```

*Dashed `- - -` in earlier diagrams marks a fake/motion ball path; the fine dash `. . .` here marks the actual forward pass, per our diagram conventions (§2, "Diagram conventions").*

### Assignments (one sentence each)

- **CENTER:** Hike the ball **up into the QB's hands** between your legs, then run five steps ahead and turn to help underneath if nobody else is open.
- **QB:** Take the snap and run the **exact ORANGE GHOST fake** — open to blue, fake to Blue Back on step 1 — but on step **2**, instead of handing to the Orange Back, **plant, come up, and throw** to ORANGE WIDE on his in-cut; **check the corner who chased the fake first** (see "The Throw" below), throw the checkdown to the Orange Back if both wides are covered, and **throw it away or run it out of bounds** if nobody is open — never eat the fake into a sack.
- **BLUE BACK:** Run the identical empty-handed sell you use on ORANGE GHOST — **sprint like you have the ball**, because the whole play depends on you looking real.
- **ORANGE BACK:** Sell the mesh with the QB exactly like you're about to take the handoff, then **release outside as the checkdown** — you are the safety valve, not a decoy who stands still.
- **BLUE WIDE:** Run the same **5-step IN** you run on every play — you are the **outlet/mirror target** if the fake instead pulls the defense toward the orange side.
- **ORANGE WIDE:** Run the same **5-step IN** you run on every play — you are the **primary target**, arriving on your break right as the QB comes up throwing.

### The throw — primary receiver and the binary rule

**Coach's request was "pass to the blue or orange receiver" — at 6-7 years old a two-way progression read is not coachable.** So we make it a single binary decision, decided by what the fake did, not by scanning both receivers:

- **Primary receiver: ORANGE WIDE.** The play is built off the ORANGE GHOST fake, which sends the Blue Back (decoy) toward the **blue** cone. A defense that bites on that fake tends to flow toward the **blue** side — which is exactly the side **away** from ORANGE WIDE, clearing his in-cut. **Default call: throw ORANGE WIDE.**
- **One-look rule for the other receiver:** *before the snap*, the QB is taught one simple visual check: **"Look at the Orange cornerback's feet. If he is already cheating toward the middle or the blue side before the snap, throw BLUE WIDE instead — the fake will pull him even further out of position on that side."** This is a **pre-snap read, not a live progression** — the QB picks his target before the ball is snapped and does not change his mind mid-play. Binary, coachable, one look, done.
- **Checkdown:** If neither wide receiver is open (both corners sat at home, or a hawk/linebacker dropped into the throwing lane), the QB's checkdown is the **Orange Back releasing to the flat** — a short, safe, low-risk throw that mirrors BLUE BUBBLE's soft toss.
- **Bail-out:** If nobody — not ORANGE WIDE, not BLUE WIDE, not the Orange Back — is open, **throw the ball away out of bounds, or run straight out of bounds if a running lane is open.** An incomplete throwaway is a loss of down; a sack, a scramble, or holding the ball past 7 seconds is worse. **We never risk the pass clock to force a throw** (Ref §8).

### Rules compliance — verified and cited

- **7-second pass clock (Ref §8): the fake must fit inside it, with room to spare.** Counted out: snap (0:00) → QB's first step and fake to Blue Back (~1 second) → QB's second step, plant (~1 more second, ~2 seconds total) → throw released (~3 seconds total). That leaves a **4-second cushion** inside the 7-second clock even before the ball is thrown — plenty of margin for a QB who hesitates or has to go through the one-look check. **Coaching point: if the QB is still holding the ball at a 4-count out loud, throw it away — do not wait for 7.**
- **QB may not run on a snap-and-go (Ref §6, §0.1):** ORANGE DART never has the QB running with the ball. His only ball actions are catch-the-snap, fake, and throw. The bail-out is "throw it away," not "tuck and run" — the QB is not a legal runner off this snap, full stop.
- **Forward pass thrown from behind the line of scrimmage (Ref §6, §8):** the QB's plant-and-throw spot on step 2 is the **same depth as the ORANGE GHOST handoff spot**, which is well behind the line of scrimmage. He never advances upfield before releasing the ball, so there is no illegal-forward-pass risk (illegal forward pass = 5 yards and loss of down, Ref §8, §11).
- **One player in motion only (Ref §7):** ORANGE DART uses **no pre-snap motion at all** — everyone is set at the snap, same as BLUE SWEEP and ORANGE GHOST. This sidesteps the motion rule entirely rather than risk an illegal-motion penalty (Ref §11) on our hardest play.
- **One foot inbounds for a completion (Ref §7):** ORANGE WIDE and BLUE WIDE must complete their in-cut and stay off the sideline the same way they're taught on every other play — drift wide on the break and an otherwise-good throw is incomplete.
- **Rushers start 7+ yards off the line (Ref §9) — the fake buys real time, but only for the rush that hasn't been triggered yet:** because BLITZ/MIKE starts 7 yards deep, he is still closing that distance while the QB's 2-step fake-and-throw happens, which is exactly why the timing above works. **⚠️ Rules note the packet does not resolve:** Ref §9 states the 7-yard rush restriction on non-rushing line defenders ends **"once the ball is handed off."** ORANGE DART's defining feature is that **the ball is never handed off** — it is faked. The reference packet is **silent** on whether a *fake* handoff releases non-rushing defenders (HAWKS/LINE) to cross the line of scrimmage the same way a real handoff does. We are **not assuming an answer either way** here: until confirmed with the league/referees, **we coach our own HAWKS/LINE defenders to treat a fake handoff as still "the ball is in the QB's hands"** (i.e., they must hold their line-of-scrimmage restriction through the fake, since the ball demonstrably has not left the QB's possession) — but we flag this explicitly as an item to confirm, exactly as Ref §16 already flags similar snap/rush ambiguities. **This also cuts both ways against us:** if an opposing coach's defenders are coached to read "handoff action = go," ORANGE DART may draw defenders across the line early on the fake, which could put our own receivers into contact sooner than expected. Coach ORANGE WIDE and BLUE WIDE to expect a defender arriving at the top of the break, not open grass.
- **No-run zones (Ref §6, §17):** ORANGE DART is a **pass**, so it is not subject to the no-run-zone restriction — it is in fact **one of our legal options inside a no-run zone**, alongside BLUE BUBBLE. Because it is our lowest-percentage pass, **BLUE BUBBLE remains the first choice in a no-run zone**; ORANGE DART is a second option there only after the defense is sitting hard on BLUE BUBBLE.

### When to call it

- **Only after BLUE SWEEP and ORANGE GHOST have both been established in a game** — the defense must already be flowing on run fakes for the play-action to mean anything. Calling this cold, before the defense respects the run, is calling our hardest play with none of its setup paid for.
- Best as a change-up on 2nd or 3rd down after the defense has started crowding the line against ORANGE GHOST specifically.
- **Not** a base call — this is a surprise play, not a rotation play. Call it sparingly.

### What makes it fail

- **The fake looks different from ORANGE GHOST.** If the QB's first two steps are even slightly off-tempo or off-depth from the real ORANGE GHOST fake, a disciplined defense (especially man corners who are taught to watch their receiver, not the backfield — see FORT, §9) will not bite, and the in-route receiver runs into a covered defender instead of open grass.
- **The QB holds the ball past the fake, hunting for a better look.** This is a one-look, binary-read play. Hesitating past the pre-snap decision burns the 7-second clock for no benefit.
- **The Orange Back stops selling the mesh and just watches.** A back who doesn't sell the fake handoff tips the whole play the instant the defense sees him stand still instead of run.
- **Forcing the throw into a covered receiver instead of taking the checkdown or throwing it away.** This is the single most common way young QBs turn our hardest play into a turnover.
- **Low completion probability generally** — see the honesty note below.

### Coaching point

**"Same first two steps as ORANGE GHOST — then plant, look Orange, throw."** To the QB: **"Fake it, plant it, one look, throw it — or throw it away."** To ORANGE WIDE: **"Same 5 steps, same break you already know — this time the ball is actually coming."** To the Orange Back: **"Sell it like you've got it, then get open — you're the safety valve, not a decoy who stops."**

### Why this only works after the run game is established

ORANGE DART has **no value in isolation** — it is entirely borrowed credibility from BLUE SWEEP and ORANGE GHOST. A defense that has never seen those plays has no reason to react to the fake, and the play degenerates into a slow-developing pass with no advantage over just calling BLUE BUBBLE. This is also the play that **finally pays off the 5-step IN route** every wide receiver has been running on every single snap since Play 1 (§2, "Wide receiver route"): that route was installed from day one specifically to drag a cornerback in and away from the sweep-and-ghost edge, and as a side effect it happens to be a fully drilled, game-ready route the moment we're ready to throw to it. ORANGE DART doesn't ask the receivers to learn anything new — it just finally throws them the ball.

### Honest risk assessment

**This is the hardest play in the book for 6-7 year olds, and it should be treated that way.** It asks a young QB to execute a clean fake, hold a one-look read, throw with timing, and hit a moving target — all inside a 7-second clock — after already succeeding at the much simpler mesh-based fake in ORANGE GHOST. **Completion probability is meaningfully lower than BLUE BUBBLE**, which only asks the QB to turn, see a receiver already facing him, and toss it softly a few feet. ORANGE DART asks for an actual forward pass, in rhythm, off misdirection.

- **Worth the risk when:** the defense has clearly bought into stopping BLUE SWEEP/ORANGE GHOST (corners and hawks crashing hard on run action), we have practice reps on the fake-then-throw timing, and the situation can absorb an incompletion (not 3rd-and-score inside a no-run zone with no downs left).
- **Not worth the risk when:** it's early in a series with downs to spare, the defense hasn't shown it respects our run game yet, or our QB of the quarter hasn't drilled the timing recently. **When in doubt, just run ORANGE GHOST or BLUE SWEEP instead** — a reliable 4 yards beats a low-percentage shot at a bigger gain.

## 7. Play 5 — BLUE FLYER

**Type: RUN — massive misdirection.** This is the hardest play in the book. It uses a fake mesh, a real handoff, a crossing decoy, and a QB decoy roll, all in the same play. **Do not install this before BLUE SWEEP, ORANGE GHOST, BLUE BUBBLE, and ORANGE DART are all reliable.** It is installed **LAST** — see §8.

### Why "FLYER"

The action word starts with **F** — unused (S, G, B, D are taken by SWEEP, GHOST, BUBBLE, DART). **FLYER** is two syllables, sounds nothing like the other four when shouted across a field, and it describes the ball's path: the Blue Back flies untouched to the edge while everybody else's eyes are somewhere else. Per our naming rule, the new action word describes the ball's path, not a person (§1). No conflict with any existing play or action word.

### The concept in one sentence

**The QB fakes to the Orange Back, then rolls right and hands off for real to the Blue Back — who runs the BLUE SWEEP path — while the Orange Back fakes away to the left, the Blue Wide runs a jet-sweep fake mesh across the formation without ever touching the ball, and the QB keeps rolling right as if he still has it.** Four different pieces of misdirection, one real ball carrier: the Blue Back.

### Rules check (verified against `Documents/FNL-Rules-Reference.md` before installing this play)

- **Center eligibility.** The Center's 3-step button route requires him to be a legal receiver after the snap. Confirmed: *"the Center has no line responsibility after the snap; he is an eligible receiver like everyone else and can release into a route or a flat/checkdown"* (Ref §0.1, "C (Center) — expanded"). Also see the general receiving rule: *"All players are eligible to receive passes"* (Ref §7). The Center's button route is legal, even though on this play he is never actually thrown the ball.
- **Blue Wide's jet path behind the line of scrimmage.** The reference does not separately restrict pre-snap or post-snap lateral motion behind the line as long as the motion rule (below) is respected. Handoffs, laterals, and pitches are explicitly allowed "as per NFL rules" (Ref §6), and nothing in the packet limits a receiver from running laterally behind the line of scrimmage as part of a route or fake. **This specific "run all the way across before turning upfield" path is not separately addressed** in the reference beyond the general motion rule — treated as legal by extension of the motion and handoff rules, but flagged here for completeness.
- **One player in motion.** *"Only one player is allowed in motion at a time"* (Ref §7; also stated at Ref §0.1, "Motion man / jet"). The **Blue Wide is our one motion man** and his jet motion **must start before the snap** to be legal pre-snap motion under this rule — no other player may be moving before the snap while Blue Wide is in motion. **Illegal Motion** (more than one person moving) costs **5 yards and loss of down** (Ref §11). Practical rule: Blue Wide goes in motion first, everyone else stays frozen, then the snap happens once he reaches a landmark depth past the Center.
- **Handoffs behind the line of scrimmage.** *"Handoffs, laterals and pitches are allowed as per NFL rules"* (Ref §6). The QB-to-Blue-Back handoff on his roll to the right is a standard behind-the-line handoff, identical in kind to the handoffs already used in BLUE SWEEP and ORANGE GHOST.
- **No pass clock on this play.** This is a **RUN** — no forward pass ever occurs. *"Once the ball is handed off, the seven second rule no longer is in effect"* (Ref §8). The 7-second pass clock does not apply here at all, and once the QB hands to the Blue Back, defenders are free to cross immediately: *"Once the ball has been handed off, lateraled or pitched, all defensive players are eligible to rush"* (Ref §6, also Ref §9).
- **QB's ball-less decoy roll.** The QB hands off, then keeps moving without the ball. This does not implicate the "QB cannot run" restriction at all, because the QB is never a runner with the ball on this play — he only ever carries it from the Center's snap to the handoff. *"The quarterback cannot run with the ball"* (Ref §6) governs a QB advancing **with** the ball; a QB moving empty-handed after a legal handoff is not "running with the ball" under any reading of that rule. This is a clean decoy action, not a rules question.
- **No-run zones.** BLUE FLYER is a **run play**, so it **is** subject to the no-running-zone restriction near either end zone and near midfield, the same as BLUE SWEEP and ORANGE GHOST (Ref §6, §17). **Do not call BLUE FLYER inside a no-run zone** — call BLUE BUBBLE or ORANGE DART there instead.
- **Not addressed in the reference — flag for league confirmation:** the reference is silent on whether a receiver may legally run a route that carries him laterally all the way across the entire formation, sideline to sideline, behind the line of scrimmage, before turning upfield (as opposed to a standard release). We believe this is legal by extension of the general motion and route rules above, since nothing prohibits the path shape of a route, but **this exact path is not explicitly addressed** in the packet. **Recommend confirming with the league** before relying on it in a game.

### BLUE FLYER diagram

```
   |            /   5 steps, then break IN                    |          |

   |                            (QB)  <- hand-to-hand snap      |        |

   |                                          ^ ^ ^  <----------+        |
   |   [OB]--fake-->. . .                (3) MESH POINT:                 |

   |   cuts LEFT,   (dashed, decoy)       BB ALREADY HAS BALL             |

   |                    BB KEEPS THE BALL, finishes the play              |
   |                                                                     |
   |                                       [BW continues past mesh,      |
   |                                        crosses ALL the way to        |

   |                                        empty-handed the whole time]  |

```

*Numbers on the diagram mark the sequence: (1) Blue Wide's jet motion starts before the snap and continues behind the line, (2) QB fakes to Orange Back who cuts away to the orange side as a decoy, (3) the fake mesh point — Blue Wide passes the Blue Back's LEFT shoulder with no exchange, timed to happen AFTER the real handoff is already complete, (4) QB rolls to the blue side, ball-less, selling the decoy. The Blue Back's real path (solid) is identical to BLUE SWEEP.*

### Assignments (one sentence each)

- **CENTER:** Hike the ball up into the QB's hands between your legs, then run **three steps straight ahead, turn around, and wait** — you are an outlet, but the ball is **never** coming to you on this play.
- **QB:** Take the snap, **fake the handoff to the Orange Back**, then **roll to the blue side and hand the ball for real to the Blue Back**, and **keep rolling to the blue side afterward with empty hands, pretending you still have the ball**.
- **BLUE BACK:** Take the **real handoff** from the QB on the roll and run the **exact same path as BLUE SWEEP** — wide to the blue cone, then turn upfield — **you keep the ball and finish the play**.
- **ORANGE BACK:** **Take the fake** from the QB, then **cut hard to the orange side** and run a path like an orange sweep — you are a full-speed decoy, not the ball carrier.
- **BLUE WIDE:** At the snap, go in **jet motion behind the line of scrimmage**, passing directly by the Blue Back's **LEFT shoulder** — **sell it like a real exchange is happening, but no ball ever changes hands** — then keep running all the way across to the orange side and turn upfield.
- **ORANGE WIDE:** Run the same **5-step IN route** you run on every play — unchanged.

### The fake mesh — the heart of the play

This is the single most important mechanic in BLUE FLYER, and it must be drilled slowly, at walking speed, for a long time before it is ever run at game speed.

- **Timing rule, non-negotiable: the QB's real handoff to the Blue Back must be fully complete — ball secured in the Blue Back's hands — BEFORE the Blue Wide arrives at the mesh point.** The Blue Back is never standing at the mesh point waiting on the ball while the Blue Wide is also arriving; the real exchange happens first, off to the side, and only then does the Blue Wide's path cross behind/near the Blue Back's position. This sequencing is what prevents a collision AND what prevents the defense from ever seeing two players contesting one football.
- **Path and spacing:** the Blue Wide's jet path passes the Blue Back's **LEFT shoulder** — meaning the Blue Wide stays on the side of the Blue Back closer to the middle of the field/QB, not the sideline side. We coach a minimum of **two full arm's lengths (roughly 3-4 feet)** of separation between the two players at the closest point of the mesh — enough that their shoulders and hands do not brush, but close enough that the fake still looks like a real exchange to a defender 5-7 yards away.
- **Selling it:** both players must **look at each other and reach/present** exactly as they would on a real exchange — Blue Back turns his near hip slightly toward Blue Wide as if offering the ball, Blue Wide reaches with both hands as if receiving — for a full beat, even though the ball is already gone. A fake that isn't sold with the eyes and hands is a fake that fools nobody.
- **No ball ever touches the Blue Wide's hands.** This bears repeating because it is the single most important safety and legality point of the whole play: there is no exchange, no bobble risk, nothing to fumble (and remember, Ref §10: **there are no fumbles** anyway) — but more importantly for coaching purposes, drilling "hands never actually meet" removes any chance of an accidental live ball ending up with the wrong runner.
- **Collision avoidance is the top safety priority for this age group.** Concretely: Blue Back's path bows out toward the sideline almost immediately off the snap (identical to his BLUE SWEEP footwork), while Blue Wide's jet path stays tighter to the line of scrimmage, several steps behind (upfield-side gap) of where Blue Back has already moved through. Because the real handoff completes first and the Blue Back is already moving away from the mesh point by the time Blue Wide arrives, the two paths are converging on a spot the Blue Back has already left — this is what makes the crossing safe as well as convincing.

### Why the misdirection works

A defense reading this play has to track **four separate things happening in four different directions at once**:
1. **The Orange Back**, faking away to the orange/left side, selling a run that direction.
2. **The Blue Wide**, jet-motioning all the way across the formation from blue to orange, a totally different kind of misdirection (lateral, not vertical).
3. **The QB**, rolling to the blue side after the handoff, still acting like a live ball carrier or passer.
4. **The Blue Back**, the actual ball carrier, moving to the blue side along a path that looks — to a defender who has to choose what to watch — like just one more moving piece among several.

A defense that keys the QB sees him roll right and has to guess whether he kept it, pitched it, or already gave it away. A defense that keys the backfield sees two backs moving in opposite directions. A defense that keys the perimeter sees a receiver sprinting across the entire width of the field. With four legitimate things to track and only six defenders, at least one or two defenders will be watching the wrong piece when the real ball carrier turns the corner.

### When to call it

- **Only after BLUE SWEEP, ORANGE GHOST, BLUE BUBBLE, and ORANGE DART are all reliable and boring.** This is the 5th and final install — see §8. Calling BLUE FLYER before the other four plays are second nature is calling our most complex play with none of the underlying mechanics (mesh timing, sweep footwork, decoy selling) already automatic.
- Best used sparingly, as a change-up once a defense has seen and started to respect our other misdirection plays (especially ORANGE GHOST).
- **Not** inside a no-run zone (Ref §6, §17) — this is a run play, so the standard no-run-zone restriction applies.

### What makes it fail

- **The QB hands off before he has fully sold the fake to the Orange Back**, tipping the defense that the real ball is going the other way.
- **The Blue Wide and Blue Back collide or fumble-fake the mesh** because the timing rule above wasn't followed — i.e., the Blue Wide arrives before the real handoff is complete, putting two players' hands near one football at the same moment.
- **The Blue Back stops or slows** anywhere along his path — since this play borrows BLUE SWEEP's footwork, any hesitation is exactly as costly here as it is on BLUE SWEEP itself.
- **The QB stops his roll immediately after the handoff** instead of continuing to sell it — a QB who "gives up" on the decoy roll the instant the ball leaves his hands wastes one-quarter of the misdirection this play is built on.
- **The Orange Back or Blue Wide jog their fakes** instead of running them at full speed — a lazy decoy is worse than no decoy, because it teaches the defense to ignore that side of the field on future snaps.
- **The Center forgets his own route entirely** and stands still — while he is never thrown the ball, a Center who doesn't run his 3-step button route removes one more thing the defense has to account for, slightly weakening the whole picture.
- This play has **more moving parts and more failure points than any other play in the book** (a fake mesh, a real handoff, a crossing player, and a decoy roll, all in the same play) — expect more blown reps in practice than any other play before it becomes reliable.

### Coaching points

- **"Ball first, THEN the cross."** Drill the timing rule above literally in that order, out loud, every single rep: QB hands to Blue Back — pause — THEN Blue Wide arrives at the mesh point. Never the reverse.
- **"Left shoulder, arm's length."** Cue phrase for the Blue Wide's path relative to the Blue Back — passes his left shoulder, stays roughly two arm's lengths away, no closer.
- **"Sell it with your hands and your eyes, not just your feet."** Both players in the fake mesh must look at each other and reach, not just run past one another — a good fake mesh looks identical to a real one from the sideline.
- **"Everybody who doesn't have the ball still has a job."** The Orange Back, Blue Wide, Center, and QB (after the handoff) all have to sprint their fakes with full conviction — this play collapses if any single decoy jogs.
- **Walk it. Then jog it. Then run it.** This play must be taught in stages — walk-through first with no contact and no speed, focusing purely on the mesh timing and spacing, for many reps before ever adding speed.

### Honest execution risk

**BLUE FLYER has the most failure points of any play in this book, and it will take longer to get right than any other play.** It layers a fake mesh, a real handoff, a crossing decoy runner, and a QB decoy roll into a single snap — any one of those four pieces breaking down (bad timing at the mesh, a hesitant Blue Back, a QB who stops selling, a decoy who jogs) is enough to blow the whole play, and there are four separate places for that to happen instead of one or two. Expect this play to require more walk-through reps than BLUE SWEEP, ORANGE GHOST, BLUE BUBBLE, and ORANGE DART combined before it is ready for a game. **When in doubt, don't call it — a reliable BLUE SWEEP or ORANGE GHOST beats a blown BLUE FLYER every time.**

### A note on future plays — this is a SERIES

The fake mesh built into BLUE FLYER is deliberately designed to leave the door open for **future plays built off the identical action**, most obviously:
- A **real** handoff to the Blue Wide on the jet motion (instead of a fake), with the Blue Back's action becoming the decoy.
- A **pass** thrown off the QB's roll-out to the blue side, using the same fake-mesh setup to hold the defense before the QB pulls up and throws instead of continuing to sell the decoy.

**We are not building either of those plays now** — this section only documents that the door is open. Any future play built off this identical action should follow our existing naming rule (§1): **same COLOR word (BLUE) + a new ACTION word starting with an unused letter** (S, G, B, D, F are taken by SWEEP, GHOST, BUBBLE, DART, FLYER — the next new action word must start with a different, still-unused letter). This keeps any future addition to this series coherent with the rest of the naming system rather than inventing a new convention just for this family of plays.

## 8. How to Teach These

We only get **one practice per week** (Ref §15), so the order matters. Do not move to the next step until the step before it is boring.

### Install order

1. **The snap — hand-to-hand, under center.** QB's hands pressed up under the Center; Center hikes the ball **up into his hands**, between the legs (Ref §6). Repeat it until it is boring. This is still the first thing we do every practice, but it is now **much lower risk than a shotgun snap** — the ball never leaves anyone's hands, so a dropped snap and the instant dead play that comes with it are close to eliminated. The remaining error is the **QB pulling out early**, so drill "hands stay until you feel it."
2. **Flags-away running.** Hands off your hips. Flag guarding costs 10 yards and the down (Ref §11).
3. **STAR alignment.** Whistle → everyone finds their spot with no coach pointing. Time them. Make it a race. **New this season:** drill the two backs' tight split-back spot specifically — practice the first step out of it so the Blue Back and Orange Back never tangle up leaving their spots.
4. **QB footwork off the snap.** No backs, no defense: snap, secure, **open left**; snap, secure, **open right**; snap, secure, **two steps straight back**. Those three turns cover BLUE SWEEP, ORANGE GHOST, and BLUE BUBBLE. With the backs tight, these turns are now **shorter and quicker** than before — drill the QB to stop his turn at the same shallow depth every time.
5. **BLUE SWEEP.** Walk it, jog it, run it — the handoff comes **even earlier** now with the tight backfield, so start slow. Then **ORANGE SWEEP** — same play, other cone.
6. **BLUE BUBBLE.** Teach the motion-and-turn first with **no ball**, then add the QB's two steps back and the soft toss. Add the 7-second count out loud so they feel the clock.
7. **ORANGE GHOST.** Needs two clean turns and a mesh point, and it needs the defense to already believe BLUE SWEEP. With the tight backfield the two meshes come **quicker and closer together** than before, so this is the play that most rewards the slow, repeatable rehearsal we already do.
8. **ORANGE DART.** Do not teach this play until the run game (steps 5 and 7) no longer needs conscious thought from any player. Teach it in stages: first, the QB and both backs walk the **identical ORANGE GHOST fake** with no throw at all, until it is genuinely indistinguishable from ORANGE GHOST at walking speed. Only then add the plant-and-throw on step 2, and only then add the one-look pre-snap rule (§6, "The throw"). Count the 7-second clock out loud on every rep — fake, plant, throw, by the 3-count — so the timing margin is drilled in from day one, not discovered live in a game.
9. **BLUE FLYER — installed LAST, the 5th and final play, only after BLUE SWEEP, ORANGE GHOST, BLUE BUBBLE, and ORANGE DART (steps 5, 7, 6, and 8) are all reliable and boring.** This is the hardest play in the book — do not rush the install. Teach it in this order: (a) the Blue Back's path alone, walking BLUE SWEEP footwork with no other moving parts; (b) add the QB's fake-to-Orange-Back and real roll-and-handoff to the Blue Back, at walking speed, with everyone else standing still, until the timing of "ball secure, THEN cross" is automatic; (c) add the Blue Wide's jet motion and the fake mesh itself, still at walking speed, drilling the left-shoulder spacing and the sell (eyes, hands) with no speed at all; (d) add the Orange Back's fake and the Center's button route; (e) only once every piece is correct at walking speed, begin jogging it, then running it at game speed. Expect this play to take longer to install than all four other plays combined.

### Small reminders for game day

- Every player must be able to run every spot — **players rotate every quarter** (Ref §2).
- Only **1 coach on the field**, so calls have to be short and fixable from where we stand (Ref §1, §17).
- **30 seconds to snap** — huddle fast, two words, break (Ref §4).
- The QB **cannot run** on a normal snap-and-go, and there are **no center sneaks** (Ref §6). Every play here moves the ball by handoff or pass, so we never have to think about it. Being under center does **not** change this — the QB still may not keep it. This includes ORANGE DART's fake — the QB fakes, plants, and throws; he never tucks and runs.
- Under center the QB has his **back to the defense** on turns and sees the rush late; that is fine because rushers start **7 yards off** (Ref §9). He does not look for the rush — he turns and gives the ball (or, on ORANGE DART, fakes the give and throws).
- Ball is spotted **at the carrier's feet** (Ref §10) — reaching the ball forward gains nothing. Just run.

## 9. Starting Base Defense: FORT (Man Coverage)

> **We start with FORT.** This is our primary, first-taught defense this season. FENCE (§10) is the alternate zone scheme we teach second, for comparison and for situations where FORT's man matchups get stretched thin. Both defenses stay in the book — see §11 for when to call which.

**Six defenders. Six jobs. One job each, and the job is a PERSON, not a patch of grass.** FORT is **man coverage**: one blitzer, two line-of-scrimmage "hawks," two cornerbacks locked onto the wide receivers, and one deep safety. Same alignment on every snap, same way STAR is our only offensive formation (Ref §17: one job per kid, every time, is what this age group can actually execute).

**The math:** 1 blitzer + 2 hawks + 2 corners + 1 safety = **6 defenders** — exactly the 6-on-6 roster the packet requires (Ref §1).

### Rules that shaped this defense (cite before you install it — read this before the alignment)

- **Rushers must start a minimum of 7 yards from the line of scrimmage when the ball is snapped** (Ref §9). A **special marker or the referee** marks that 7 yards on the field — we call this the **7-YARD MARK**, and it is the single most important landmark in this defense.
- **Any number of players can rush the QB** (Ref §9) — the packet does not cap it. We choose **exactly one rusher** (BLITZ) on purpose: one blitzer is one clear job, and constant pressure from a single, well-drilled blitzer is more coachable than a crowd rush. Using exactly one blitzer is a **deliberate simplicity choice, not a rule requirement** — nothing stops us from adding a second rusher later if we want to.
- **BLITZ must start on or behind the 7-yard mark**, never closer (Ref §9). Same landmark discipline as everything else in this book — toes on or behind the mark, every snap.
- **HAWKS hold the line of scrimmage but may NOT cross until the ball leaves the QB's hands** (Ref §9, Ref §0.2). The instant the ball is **handed off**, that restriction ends and the HAWK is free to attack downhill.
- **CORNERS play straight man coverage** — one cornerback per wide receiver, mirroring his release and staying attached through the route.
- **SAFETY** is the deepest defender, plays the **middle of the field**, and is the backstop behind everyone when the offense shows only 2 wide receivers.
- **Illegal rushing** (BLITZ starting inside the 7-yard marker) costs **10 yards and an automatic first down** (Ref §11) — the single most costly and most avoidable mistake in this defense.
- **Offsides** (a HAWK crossing the line before the ball is handed off) costs **5 yards and an automatic first down** (Ref §11).
- **NO BLOCKING, TACKLING, OR STRIPPING OF THE BALL IS ALLOWED** (Ref §9) — every defender's only real job is **pulling the flag** (Ref §0.2).
- **Illegal Flag Pull** — pulling the flag before the receiver has the ball — costs **10 yards and an automatic first down** (Ref §11).

### The 6 spots

| # | Spot | Depth | Job in one sentence (kid language) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **BLITZ** | **On or behind the 7-YARD MARK** | Start on the 7-yard mark and sprint straight at the QB on every snap. |
| 2 | **BLUE HAWK** | On the line of scrimmage | Stand your ground on the blue side of the line — don't cross until the ball leaves the QB's hands. |
| 3 | **ORANGE HAWK** | On the line of scrimmage | Stand your ground on the orange side of the line — don't cross until the ball leaves the QB's hands. |
| 4 | **BLUE CB** | Across from the blue-side receiver | Stick to your one receiver like glue — go where he goes. |
| 5 | **ORANGE CB** | Across from the orange-side receiver | Stick to your one receiver like glue — go where he goes. |
| 6 | **SAFETY** | Deepest, middle | You own the deep middle grass — nobody gets behind you, ever. |

The offense's own STAR formation lines up just below the line of scrimmage, facing up into this defense.*

### Man coverage, in kid language

- **Man at this age means one simple rule: "Pick your person before the snap. Go where he goes. Don't leave him for anything."**
- **BLUE CB** covers whoever lines up as **BLUE WIDE**. **ORANGE CB** covers whoever lines up as **ORANGE WIDE**.
- **SAFETY's job when the offense shows only 2 WRs:** stay deepest, stay in the middle, and be the backstop if a corner gets beat. Nobody gets behind the safety, ever.
- **BLUE HAWK / ORANGE HAWK's grass is the line of scrimmage itself** — hold it, don't cross until the ball is gone, then attack whoever has the ball.

### Adjustment rules

- **Offense shows 3+ receivers:** the **SAFETY converts to man on the 3rd receiver**, leaving the deep field open. This is an **explicit, accepted tradeoff at this age** — we would rather have a body on every receiver than a deep defender covering nobody. Coaching note: this is FORT's honest limitation, and it is the main reason we also carry FENCE (§10) for spread-out offenses.
- **Offense shows fewer than 2 WRs:** the **spare CB shifts inside** to help contain the run. **SAFETY always stays home deep**, no matter how few receivers are out — the deep field is never left completely uncovered on purpose.

### Coaching points and common failure modes

- **BLITZ lining up too close.** Starting inside the 7-yard mark is **illegal rushing: 10 yards and an automatic first down** for the other team (Ref §9, §11). Drill it exactly like a landmark: "toes on or behind the mark, every single snap, no exceptions."
- **HAWKS crossing early.** Crossing the line before the ball leaves the QB's hands is **offsides: 5 yards, automatic first down** (Ref §11). Cue: *"Hold your ground until the ball is gone."*
- **Corners chasing the backfield fake instead of their man.** The single most common man-coverage mistake at this age — a corner's eyes go to the ball fake instead of staying on his receiver, and his man gets wide open. Cue: *"Eyes on your guy, not on the fake."*
- **SAFETY jumping run action too fast.** The Safety's first read must be pass before he ever helps against the run; jumping run action early is exactly how a team gives up the one deep completion they can't otherwise get to at this age. Cue: *"Pass first. Come down last."*
- **No contact anywhere on the field.** No blocking, no tackling, no stripping the ball (Ref §9) — every defender's job is a **flag pull**, never contact. Pulling the flag **before** the receiver has secured the catch is a penalty on us (**Illegal Flag Pull**, Ref §11).

### Why this defense (man, at this age, with constant pressure)

- **Constant pressure exploits the 7-second pass clock** (Ref §8). BLITZ attacking on every snap means the QB never gets a clean, comfortable pocket — and because the pressure comes from a fixed, legal depth (the 7-yard mark), it is completely repeatable and never risks the illegal-rushing penalty as long as the landmark is respected.
- **One job per kid, six single-sentence assignments** — same teaching philosophy as our one-formation offense (Ref §17), just applied to a person to cover instead of a patch of grass.
- **The packet permits any number of rushers** (Ref §9) — using exactly one blitzer here is a deliberate simplicity choice, not a rule requirement, same as it is in FENCE.

### How FORT defends our own plays (for scrimmage use)

- **BLUE SWEEP / ORANGE GHOST (run plays to the edge):** **BLUE HAWK and ORANGE HAWK** are exactly where a sweep has to go — they hold the edge without crossing early, then the instant the ball is handed off, the restriction is gone and they can attack (Ref §9). Because HAWKS are tight to the line (not 5 yards deep like FENCE's THIRDS), FORT's man corners tend to defend our own sweeps and ghost fakes tighter than a zone look does — there is less of a seam at the edge for a fast ball carrier to slip through before a defender disengages.
- **BLUE BUBBLE (pass, motion + short toss):** BLITZ directly tests the QB's two-steps-back-then-throw window inside the 7-second clock (Ref §8). Because **BLUE CB** is locked man-to-man on **BLUE WIDE**, not on the Blue Back running the bubble motion, the corner may not be the first defender to react to the short toss — whichever defender picks up the motion man (often the SAFETY or a HAWK converting late) has to identify and close on him without a natural zone landmark to lean on. This is the one spot where FENCE's zone assignment (§9) reacts a little more naturally than FORT's man rule.
- **ORANGE DART (play-action pass off the sweep/ghost fake):** FORT's man corners stay locked on their receivers regardless of the run fake, so **BLUE CB/ORANGE CB** do not bite on backfield action the way a zone defender's eyes might — this makes ORANGE DART's primary read (whichever corner the fake pulls off his landmark) less reliable against FORT than against FENCE, since a disciplined man corner is taught to ignore the fake entirely. **BLITZ** is still the biggest threat to the 7-second timing, since he is sprinting from the 7-yard mark the instant the ball is snapped, fake or not.

## 10. Alternate Defense: FENCE (2-3-1 Zone)

**Six defenders. Six jobs. One job each — but now the job is a PATCH OF GRASS, not a person.** FENCE is our **alternate** defense, taught second and used to compare against FORT (§9) or to counter spread-out offenses that stress FORT's man coverage. FENCE is a **2-3-1 zone**: 2 line defenders, 3 linebackers (one of whom blitzes), 1 deep safety. Same alignment on every snap, same way STAR is our only offensive formation (Ref §17: one job per kid, every time, is what this age group can actually execute).

**The math:** 2 line + 3 linebackers + 1 safety = **6 defenders** — exactly the 6-on-6 roster the packet requires (Ref §1).

### Rules that shaped this defense (cite before you install it — read this before the alignment)

- **Rushers must start a minimum of 7 yards from the line of scrimmage when the ball is snapped** (Ref §9). A **special marker or the referee** marks that 7 yards on the field — we call this the **7-YARD MARK**, and it is the single most important landmark in this defense.
- **Any number of players can rush the QB** (Ref §9) — the packet does not cap it. We choose **exactly one rusher** (the MIKE) on purpose: one blitzer is one clear job, and constant pressure from a single, well-drilled blitzer is more coachable than a crowd rush.
- ⚠️ **RULING — the MIKE's depth.** Any blitzer is a rusher under the packet. Therefore the **MIKE cannot line up at a conventional 3-4 yard linebacker depth** — he **must** start **on or behind the 7-yard mark**, exactly like BLITZ does in FORT (§9) (Ref §9). We make this the single most obvious landmark on the field for him: same treatment as FORT's blitzer.
- ⚠️ **RULING — the two outside linebackers (zone thirds).** These two are **NOT rushing the QB** — they are zone defenders. The 7-yard rule in Ref §9 applies **only to rushers**. Non-rushing defenders **may defend at the line of scrimmage, but only until the ball leaves the QB's hands**; once the ball is **handed off**, that restriction ends and everyone may cross (Ref §9, Ref §0.2). So the two outside linebackers are free to play any depth that makes zone sense (we use ~5 yards, deep enough to see the whole field) — they are not required to be at 7 yards, because they are not rushers.
- ⚠️ **RULING — the "2 rushers on the line of scrimmage."** This phrase is **not legal as a literal rush**: a defender **cannot both start on the line of scrimmage and rush the QB**, because rushers must start 7 yards back (Ref §9). We resolve this the way closest to the intent: the 2 line players are **NOT rushers**. They are **line-of-scrimmage defenders** (same legal role as FORT's "HAWKS," §9) who **hold the line and cannot cross until the ball leaves the QB's hands** (Ref §9, Ref §0.2). Once the ball is snapped and in the QB's hands, they read run/pass at the line; the instant it's handed off, they're free to attack. This gets us "2 defenders sitting right on the line" without committing an illegal rush.
- **Illegal rushing** (a rusher starting inside the 7-yard marker — this means our MIKE, specifically) costs **10 yards and an automatic first down** (Ref §11) — the single most costly and most avoidable mistake in this defense.
- **Offsides** (a non-rushing defender — a line defender or a zone linebacker — crossing the line early) costs **5 yards and an automatic first down** (Ref §11).
- **NO BLOCKING, TACKLING, OR STRIPPING OF THE BALL IS ALLOWED** (Ref §9) — every defender's only real job is **pulling the flag** (Ref §0.2).
- **Illegal Flag Pull** — pulling the flag before the receiver has the ball — costs **10 yards and an automatic first down** (Ref §11).

### The 6 spots

| # | Spot | Depth | Job in one sentence (kid language) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **BLUE LINE** | On the line of scrimmage | Stand your ground on the blue side of the line — don't cross until the ball leaves the QB's hands. |
| 2 | **ORANGE LINE** | On the line of scrimmage | Stand your ground on the orange side of the line — don't cross until the ball leaves the QB's hands. |
| 3 | **BLUE THIRD** | ~5 yards deep, blue side | This sideline to the middle cone is your grass — nobody catches or runs through your box. |
| 4 | **ORANGE THIRD** | ~5 yards deep, orange side | This sideline to the middle cone is your grass — nobody catches or runs through your box. |
| 5 | **MIKE** (blitzer) | **On or behind the 7-YARD MARK** | Start on the 7-yard mark and sprint straight at the QB on every snap. |
| 6 | **SAFETY** | Deepest, middle | You own the deep middle grass — nobody gets behind you, ever. |

*Downfield is still up the page (same convention as every offensive diagram). The `//// ////` bands mark the three zone thirds so the kids can see them drawn out; on the real field these are simple cone landmarks, not painted lines. The offense's own STAR formation lines up just below this line of scrimmage, facing up into this defense.*

### Zone landmarks (kid language — "cover your part of the grass")

- **Zone at this age does not mean pattern-matching or reading routes.** It means: *"Stay in your box. If somebody comes into your box, go pull their flag. If nobody comes into your box, stay in your box."*
- We plant **two extra landmark cones** at practice, one on each side, roughly a third of the way in from each sideline, at the depth the linebackers play. Those cones are the inside edge of **BLUE THIRD** and **ORANGE THIRD**.
- **BLUE THIRD's grass:** from the **blue sideline** to the **blue landmark cone**.
- **ORANGE THIRD's grass:** from the **orange sideline** to the **orange landmark cone**.
- **SAFETY's grass:** everything **behind and between** the two landmark cones, all the way deep. The Safety is the backstop for both thirds and for the deep middle.
- **BLUE LINE / ORANGE LINE's grass:** the line of scrimmage itself, blue side and orange side — hold it, don't cross until the ball is gone.

### Adjustment rules

- **Offense shows 3+ receivers:** BLUE THIRD and ORANGE THIRD still each own their sideline-to-cone box — a zone defense doesn't need extra defenders for extra receivers, since every zone defender pulls the flag of **whoever enters their box**, not a specific jersey. The **SAFETY still owns the deep middle** and does not lock onto a single receiver. *Coaching note: this is one of zone's built-in advantages over FORT's man scheme (§9) — we no longer run out of defenders when the offense spreads us out.*
- **Offense shows a heavy run look (extra backs, no wides):** **BLUE LINE and ORANGE LINE** stay put and hold the edge — they are our first line against the run. **BLUE THIRD and ORANGE THIRD** are told explicitly: *"Nobody in your box yet — you can help crash down on the run, but only after the ball is handed off"* (Ref §9: once handed off, all defenders may cross). The **MIKE's blitz** is already attacking downhill through the middle, which is exactly where a heavy run look wants to go.
- **Near our own goal line:** **SAFETY tightens up** — the deep middle is now much shorter, so the Safety can afford to sit closer to the play without giving up a big pass over the top. **BLUE THIRD and ORANGE THIRD tighten their boxes toward the sideline cones** (the field is shorter, so the thirds get smaller too) and **must not let anyone get outside them** — there is no room behind them to recover.

### Coaching points and common failure modes

- **The MIKE lining up too close.** The single most common and most costly error in this defense — starting inside the 7-yard mark is **illegal rushing: 10 yards and an automatic first down** for the other team (Ref §9, §11). Drill it exactly like a landmark: "toes on or behind the mark, every single snap, no exceptions." Same #1 rule as BLITZ in FORT (§9).
- **Zone defenders drifting out of their third, or chasing the ball instead of guarding grass.** This is the #1 new failure mode with a zone. A BLUE THIRD or ORANGE THIRD defender who runs toward the ball (instead of staying in his box until someone enters it) leaves his own third wide open for the very next play. Cue: *"Grass first, ball second. If nobody's in your box, stay in your box."*
- **Line defenders (BLUE LINE / ORANGE LINE) crossing early.** They are not rushers — crossing the line before the ball leaves the QB's hands is **offsides: 5 yards, automatic first down** (Ref §11). Cue: *"Hold your ground until the ball is gone."*
- **The SAFETY coming up too fast on run action.** The Safety's first read must be pass before he ever helps against the run; jumping run action early is exactly how a team gives up the one deep completion they can't otherwise get to at this age. Cue: *"Pass first. Come down last."*
- **No contact anywhere on the field.** No blocking, no tackling, no stripping the ball (Ref §9) — every defender's job is a **flag pull**, never contact. Pulling the flag **before** the receiver has secured the catch is a penalty on us (**Illegal Flag Pull**, Ref §11).

### Why this defense (zone, at this age, with constant pressure)

- **Zone assigns a patch of grass, not a moving player.** A 6-7 year old can hold still and watch a box far more reliably than he can track and stay attached to a moving person for an entire play. "Stay in your box and pull whoever comes in" is a simpler, more repeatable job than "follow this one kid everywhere he goes."
- **Constant middle pressure exploits the 7-second pass clock** (Ref §8). The MIKE blitzing on every snap means the QB never gets a clean, comfortable pocket — and because the pressure comes from a fixed, legal depth (the 7-yard mark), it is completely repeatable and never risks the illegal-rushing penalty as long as the landmark is respected.
- **One job per kid, six single-sentence assignments** — same teaching philosophy as our one-formation offense (Ref §17), just applied to zone boxes instead of man matchups.

### How FENCE defends our own plays (for scrimmage use)

- **BLUE SWEEP / ORANGE GHOST (run plays to the edge):** **BLUE LINE and ORANGE LINE** are exactly where a sweep has to go — they hold the edge without crossing early, then the instant the ball is handed off, the restriction is gone and they can attack (Ref §9). **This is our defense's honest weak point against our own sweep game:** BLUE THIRD/ORANGE THIRD are zone defenders playing ~5 yards deep, not tight to the line, so there is a real gap between the line defender and the zone linebacker on the same side — a fast sweep that turns the corner before the line defender disengages can get into that gap before either the third-defender or the Safety can close. Our own 5-step-IN receiver route makes this worse for an opponent's defense (it's designed to pull a corner inside), but it does **not** fix this gap for us — a zone at this level simply has less beef at the edge than FORT's tight man-corner alignment (§8) does. Coaching answer: BLUE THIRD/ORANGE THIRD must **squeeze down** the instant they see an outside run and the MIKE's blitz angle should bend toward ball flow rather than run straight upfield once the ball is out.
- **BLUE BUBBLE (pass, motion + short toss):** The **MIKE's blitz** directly tests the QB's two-steps-back-then-throw window inside the 7-second clock (Ref §8). **BLUE THIRD** owns the flat where the Blue Back sets up — since it's a zone, BLUE THIRD doesn't need to identify the motion man specifically, just that someone has entered his box, so this matchup is actually **easier** for a zone defense than it is for FORT's man corner (§9) tied to a specific receiver. The **SAFETY** reads pass immediately (QB stepping backward is a pass key) but should not crash forward on the short toss — the deep middle stays his job in case the catch turns into a longer run than expected.
- **ORANGE DART (play-action pass off the sweep/ghost fake):** **BLUE THIRD/ORANGE THIRD** are the defenders most likely to bite on the run fake, since their job is "read your box and react," and a hard sweep/ghost fake toward their box can pull them up before they see the receiver arriving behind them — this is FENCE's honest vulnerability to play action. The **SAFETY** is the backstop: his rule ("pass first, come down last") is exactly what keeps this from being a explosive play against zone. The **MIKE's** blitz from the 7-yard mark is unaffected by the fake and remains the primary timing threat inside the 7-second clock.

## 11. FORT vs. FENCE — Which to Call

Both defenses stay in the book all season. They are not a "before and after" — they are two tools for two different situations, and the coach will teach both and compare them in practice.

| | **FORT (man)** | **FENCE (zone)** |
|---|---|---|
| Assignment | One person to cover | One patch of grass to cover |
| Best against | Tight sets, edge runs (BLUE SWEEP / ORANGE GHOST) | Spread sets, BLUE BUBBLE |
| 3+ receivers | SAFETY must convert to man on the 3rd WR — **deep field opens up** | No adjustment needed — thirds just cover whoever enters the box |
| Edge run defense | Tight — CBs/HAWKS are right on top of receivers and the edge | Looser — 5-yard-deep THIRD leaves a seam next to the LINE defender |
| Biggest honest weakness | Deep field exposed once the offense shows 3+ WRs | Edge seam between LINE and THIRD on a fast sweep |

### The simple decision rule

- **If the offense lines up with 2 or fewer wide receivers, start in FORT.** Tight man coverage on the edge is the better answer to our opponents' likely run-heavy, low-WR-count sets at this age, and FORT's one real weakness (the 3rd-receiver adjustment) never comes into play.
- **If the offense spreads out with 3 or more wide receivers, switch to FENCE.** FENCE's zone thirds don't care how many receivers show up — nobody has to abandon the deep middle the way FORT's Safety does.
- **When in doubt, or early in the season while both are still being taught, start in FORT** (per the head coach's instruction) and switch to FENCE if the offense starts consistently showing 3+ receivers or working the middle of the field successfully against FORT's man corners.
- **Neither defense is "better" — they trade the same weakness for a different one:** FORT gives up the deep middle against spread sets; FENCE gives up the edge seam against sweeps. Pick based on what the offense in front of you is showing.
